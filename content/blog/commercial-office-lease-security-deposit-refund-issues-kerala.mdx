## 1. The Anatomy of a Commercial Lease Refund Dispute

When a business decides to downsize, relocate, or close a regional branch office, recovering the security deposit frequently encounters three common landlord maneuvers:

### A. The "Restoration to Bare-Shell" Deduction
Most commercial tenancy agreements contain a standard clause requiring the tenant to *"hand over the demised premises in its original condition, reasonable wear and tear excepted."*
- **The Landlord's Tactic:** Landlords often claim that partition removal, minor drywall holes, air conditioning ducting, or paint scuffs exceed "normal wear and tear." They present inflated quotes from their preferred local contractors and deduct ₹1 Lakh to ₹3 Lakhs from the refundable balance.

### B. Indefinite Delay ("Will Pay When the Next Tenant Moves In")
A widespread practice among commercial property owners in Kerala is treating tenant security deposits as capital to fund personal investments or building construction rather than keeping the funds in an escrow or liquid reserve.
- **The Trap:** When you serve your 60-day notice to vacate, the landlord reveals they do not have the liquid cash to repay ₹8 Lakhs immediately, promising repayment *"as soon as a new tenant leases the floor."* Tenants often wait 6 to 14 months to recover their own money.

### C. Rigid Lock-In Period Forfeiture
Conventional commercial leases feature a mandatory 11-month to 36-month lock-in period. If your company experiences a market downturn, team restructuring, or shifts to remote work before the lock-in expires, landlords invoke strict forfeiture clauses, seizing 100% of the advance deposit.

## 2. What Does the Law Say? (The Legal Reality in Kerala Courts)

When negotiations break down, business owners frequently consult corporate lawyers to file recovery suits. However, legal enforcement carries heavy practical drawbacks:

| Legal Action Course | Governing Statute / Procedure | Realistic Timeline in India | Practical Feasibility for Businesses |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Legal Notice by Advocate** | Section 106 Transfer of Property Act, 1882 | 15 to 30 Days | Effective only if landlord fears reputational damage; low success rate. |
| **Commercial Court Summary Suit** | Order XXXVII of Civil Procedure Code (CPC) | **18 to 36 Months** | High legal fees; landlord files leave-to-defend raising fictitious damages. |
| **Arbitration Proceedings** | Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 | **12 to 24 Months** | Expensive arbitrator fees often exceed 20% to 30% of the deposit amount. |
| **Police / Criminal Complaint** | Section 406 / 420 Indian Penal Code (BNS) | Not Applicable | Police routinely treat rent disputes as "civil matters" and refuse to file an FIR. |

Because litigation takes years and consumes substantial legal fees, corporate tenants often end up accepting a compromise: forfeiting 25% to 40% of their deposit simply to get the remaining balance returned without endless court dates.
